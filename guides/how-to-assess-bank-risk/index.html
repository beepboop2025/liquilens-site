How to assess bank risk from public financial disclosures

By LiquiLens · Published and source-checked 4 October 2026 · Educational research

Assess bank risk by reading asset quality, capital, earnings, funding and liquidity together, using comparable reporting dates and the original disclosures. Trace changes in the underlying amounts before interpreting ratios. A public-data screen can identify questions for further review; it cannot establish that a bank will fail or that a deposit is safe.

Start with the institution and the reporting perimeter

Identify the legal entity, jurisdiction, reporting date and whether the accounts cover the bank alone or a consolidated group. A parent-company balance sheet and a regulated bank subsidiary are different objects. Keep the filing publication date and any amendment date beside the period-end date.

For US banks, the FFIEC Uniform Bank Performance Report provides a useful official framework for analysing earnings, liquidity, capital and balance-sheet composition. Its user guide explains individual measures. For another jurisdiction, use the institution's filings and that jurisdiction's definitions; a familiar label does not make two ratios equivalent.

What should a bank-risk review contain?

Questions to carry into the source documents
AreaRead togetherQuestion to resolve
Asset qualityProblem-loan amounts, new problem loans, provisions, write-offs and recoveriesDid borrowers improve, or did accounting movements reduce the reported balance?
CapitalCapital amounts, risk-weighted assets, leverage and disclosed lossesWhat changed in the buffer and its denominator?
EarningsRecurring income, funding costs, credit costs and exceptional itemsWould earnings still cover costs without one-off gains?
FundingDeposit or borrowing mix, concentration, maturity and costWhich funding sources could leave or need refinancing together?
LiquidityAvailable cash, liquid assets, encumbrance and dated obligationsAre resources usable when the obligations fall due?

This is a research checklist, not a supervisory rating model. The Basel Liquidity Coverage Ratio standard, for example, addresses a specified 30-day stress horizon using high-quality liquid assets and net cash outflows. It does not make every asset immediately available or resolve every institution-specific funding risk. Check local implementation before interpreting a regulatory ratio.

Worked example: a lower bad-loan ratio can conceal an unchanged balance

Hypothetical teaching example; not an observation about a real bank. An institution reports 100 units of gross non-performing loans against 1,000 units of loans: a 10% ratio. Next period it still reports 100 problem-loan units, but total loans grow to 1,250. The ratio falls to 8% without a reduction in the problem-loan amount.

A movement statement could reconcile as: 100 opening + 40 additions − 15 recoveries − 5 upgrades − 20 write-offs = 100 closing. Recoveries, upgrades and write-offs represent different events. Preserve those distinctions instead of describing the whole reduction as borrower repayment. Use the exact categories and scope in the filing.

The useful follow-up is whether new problem loans, provisioning, growth and funding changed together. The ratio alone cannot answer that question. Equally, a higher ratio may reflect a shrinking denominator rather than a matching increase in new defaults.

How do you compare banks without mixing evidence?

  1. Match periods and definitions. Compare the same reporting quarter and compatible measures. Record remaining differences in business mix, accounting and consolidation.
  2. Read the amounts behind the ratios. Record numerator, denominator, units and any restatement.
  3. Separate missing from zero. An absent liquidity disclosure is an unanswered question, not evidence of a cash shortfall or a comfortable buffer.
  4. Preserve what was knowable. A filing published in May cannot support a decision supposedly made in March. A later amended report is not automatically the original information set.
  5. Write a bounded conclusion. Name the observed change, competing explanations, unresolved evidence and next review date.
