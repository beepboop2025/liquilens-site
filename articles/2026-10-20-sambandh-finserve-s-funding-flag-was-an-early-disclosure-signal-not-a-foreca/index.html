Sambandh Finserve’s funding flag was an early disclosure signal, not a forecast

A historical replay places Sambandh Finserve’s first fragile funding signal before its recorded default. The evidence identifies a disclosed wholesale-leverage vulnerability, while fraud masking and construction-PIT timing limit what that chronology can establish.

1410 words · institution risk · open evidence

*This is a historical replay, not current news. It is not a forecast, a credit rating, or investment advice. The filing period discussed below ended on 2016-03-31; the knowledge-time proxy is 2016-05-30. Sambandh Finserve’s recorded default date is 2020-10-13.*

The narrow answer to LiquiLens’s desk question is that Sambandh Finserve was the balance sheet that warranted earlier scrutiny because a disclosed funding measure crossed the published fragile threshold. That is not a claim that the filing predicted default, identified its cause, or created a tradeable warning. It is a claim about what the disclosed liability structure showed under a stated rule before the recorded event.

The funding lens placed Sambandh Finserve at 78.7, in the fragile band, for the period ending 2016-03-31. Its recorded flag was wholesale leverage of 8.51x against a 7x threshold. The replay calculates a 52-month lead to the recorded default. That interval is a reconstructed filing-period chronology using a knowledge-time proxy. It is neither a market-price signal nor a model-derived probability of failure.

The record before the event

The first funding signal belongs to the 2016-03-31 reporting period and is treated as known on 2016-05-30. Where an explicit publication clock is unavailable, the construction uses period end plus 60 days. That is more cautious than assuming a balance-sheet date was instantly available, but it remains a proxy rather than a verified contemporaneous information set.

The record is classified as PERIOD_END_PROXY_CONSTRUCTION_PIT. It is not eligible for validated backtesting, is not eligible for real-money use, and has no bitemporal input contract. Potential lead times may therefore be optimistic. The 52-month result is best read as a reconstructed chronology of a disclosed ratio, not evidence that a lender or investor could have acted with the same timing, information, or confidence.

A more serious limitation is that Sambandh Finserve is fraud-masked. The dossier warns that institutions whose filings were later shown to be falsified can appear compliant to a threshold engine. A mechanical screen cannot retrieve undisclosed facts or correct untruthful disclosures. This makes the case useful for testing the limits of a visible funding flag, but unsuitable for claiming that the flag explained, detected, or forecast the eventual outcome.

The underlying materials are available through the failure-radar board, the dedicated Sambandh Finserve replay, and the broader replay archive.

What the lenses saw

The funding lens saw wholesale leverage above its published threshold. That is a filing-period observation: a rule applied to a disclosed liability-side measure. It is not evidence of observed lender behaviour, refinancing prices, or market consensus. It also is not a model derivation of a calibrated failure likelihood.

The PCA lens does not provide an earlier action-zone finding for Sambandh Finserve. No first action zone or related lead period is recorded. That absence cannot be converted into evidence that the institution was safe, that supervisory thresholds cleared it, or that the funding lens was sufficient on its own.

Across the replay, the PCA summary covers 15 failed institutions; 5 entered an action zone first, with a median lead of 41 months. The funding summary also covers 15 failed institutions, but liability disclosures exist for only 10. The funding signal fired first for 4, with a median lead of 38 months. These are descriptive results from a restricted disclosure record, not prospective validation. The historical validation record retains those restrictions.

The India diagnostic covers 48 institutions across two decades and reports 88.9% of non-fraud failures flagged, with a median lead of 21.5 months. It carries the same construction-PIT, non-real-money, and non-validated limitations. Sambandh’s fraud-masked status matters especially because the headline excludes the category in which disclosure-based thresholds can be most misleading.

Why the warning mattered

Wholesale leverage matters because it raises a balance-sheet question about refinancing dependence. If asset performance weakens, earnings and capital capacity may be less able to absorb losses. Where wholesale liabilities are important, funding terms and rollover willingness may become more consequential. That is a plausible pressure chain, not an established account of what happened at Sambandh.

The flag therefore mattered as a prompt for harder investigation of funding concentration, maturity structure, liquidity resilience, lender diversity, and loss-absorption capacity. The fragile label does not demonstrate that a lender withdrew, repriced, shortened, or refused funding. The dossier supplies a threshold flag, not lender-by-lender rollover evidence, cash-flow history, or a verified transmission sequence.

This distinction is essential. A filing signal can reveal a disclosed structural exposure. A market-price signal would require authorized evidence of repricing or drawdown. Neither establishes causation. A model derivation would require an eligible prospective framework able to translate inputs into a validated probability. This record explicitly does not meet that standard.

The strongest counter-case

The counter-case can defeat an overconfident thesis. Wholesale funding is not inherently fatal. An institution can carry substantial wholesale liabilities while asset quality, capital capacity, liquidity buffers, maturities, funding diversity, and lender relationships remain resilient. A ratio threshold is a coarse representation of a more complex balance sheet.

The historical model evidence is not decisive either. The hazard panel contains 205 rows, 9 events, and 27 institutions, while 179 censored or unusable rows were excluded. Its leave-one-institution-out AUC is 0.752, with a 95% interval from 0.338 to 1.0. The heuristic comparator reports 0.799 on the same held-out rows. The temporal diagnostic AUC is 0.645, below the 0.65 gate, and remains diagnostic only.

A sceptic can reasonably conclude that the flag marked a broad vulnerability rather than a dependable countdown. Fraud masking intensifies the objection: incomplete or false disclosures can make the relationship between reported leverage and actual condition unknowable. The defensible conclusion is limited to this: Sambandh’s disclosed funding profile justified more investigation than a comparable profile without the flag.

The next falsifiable test

The thesis weakens if prospectively timestamped, vetted disclosures show flagged institutions retaining refinancing access, liquidity resilience, and loss-absorption capacity through stress while comparable unflagged institutions deteriorate first. It strengthens only if original filing vintages, explicit publication clocks, liability composition, and later outcomes are preserved before results are known.

That test cannot be satisfied by extending a retrospective replay. It requires a pre-specified prospective cohort and evaluation that does not inherit revised knowledge from later filings or outcomes.

Sources, method, and limits

This article uses the supplied LiquiLens record only: the failure-radar board, historical validation, market evidence, and Sambandh replay.

The decisive limitations are the period-end-plus-60-days availability proxy, absent bitemporal input control, potentially optimistic lead times, missing liability disclosures, fraud masking, and the absence of authorized market-derived display or tier evidence. The finding is not that Sambandh’s end was forecast. It is that a disclosed funding vulnerability appeared early enough to warrant harder questions, while the record remains too constrained to claim more.
