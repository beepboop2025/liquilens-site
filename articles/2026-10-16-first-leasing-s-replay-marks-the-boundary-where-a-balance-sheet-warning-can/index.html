First Leasing’s replay marks the boundary where a balance-sheet warning can fail

The case does not identify current default risk or establish a timely historical signal. It shows why a threshold screen cannot treat apparently compliant disclosures as proof of safety when the underlying record is fraud-masked.

1438 words · institution risk · open evidence

*This is a historical replay, not current news and not a forecast. Similar mechanisms do not imply the same outcome. First Leasing Company Of India defaulted on 2013-12-02; the current Failure Radar board is dated 2026-10-16, the market layer is as of 2026-10-15, and publication is dated 2026-10-16.*

The answer to the desk question is deliberately uncomfortable. This record does not show that First Leasing’s balance sheet should have felt financial stress first. It shows a harder proposition: a disclosure-driven balance-sheet screen can appear reassuring when its source disclosures fail to represent the condition the screen is meant to measure.

First Leasing is marked fraud-masked. Its PCA replay has no first action-zone entry and no lead-time observation. Its scoreable funding lens has no first signal and no lead-time observation. Those absences are not evidence of resilience. They establish only that these mechanical lenses did not document an earlier warning in the supplied replay.

The record before the event

First Leasing Company Of India is classified as an NBFC, with a recorded default date of 2013-12-02. The fraud-masked designation is central to interpretation, but it should not be inflated into a complete reconstruction of the event. The validation material says filings later shown falsified can look compliant to a threshold engine; the forensic screen owns that problem.

This historical evidence is classified as PERIOD_END_PROXY_CONSTRUCTION_PIT. It is not eligible for validated backtesting or real-money use, has no bitemporal input contract, and uses a 60-day filing-lag assumption when an explicit publication clock is unavailable. Availability is assigned using an explicit publication clock when present, otherwise period end plus 60 days. Lead times are explicitly optimistic.

The dossier provides neither a separate filing as-of date nor a line-by-line First Leasing filing history. That missing evidence must remain missing. It cannot be converted into a claim that an unobserved disclosure was timely, complete, or benign. Readers can examine the First Leasing replay alongside the wider LiquiLens replay archive.

What the lenses saw

Neither the PCA lens nor the funding lens produced a documented first signal for First Leasing. In a conventional screen, no breach might be treated as benign. Here it cannot carry that meaning. A PCA or supervisory-action lens responds to disclosed tripwires. A funding lens sees only institutions that disclose a liability series. Neither independently verifies the truthfulness of its inputs.

The funding limitation is observable in the historical summary: 10 of 15 failed institutions had liability disclosures. The PCA summary reports that 5 of 15 failed institutions entered an action zone first, with a median lead of 41 months. The funding summary reports 4 first funding signals, with a median lead of 38 months. These are descriptive replay results, not promises that either lens will warn on every institution or every failure.

Their timing also inherits the explicit publication-clock rule or the 60-day proxy. These are filing-period signals under a construction-PIT framework. They are not market-price signals, and they do not prove tradable advance knowledge. The historical validation record preserves those eligibility limits.

Why the warning mattered

The analytical chain is narrow. A threshold model can classify only disclosed balance-sheet conditions. If disclosed asset quality, capital, liquidity, or liabilities do not represent underlying claims, the model may not fire. A clean result can mean no disclosed breach; it cannot prove no economic breach.

That distinction separates observation from derivation. A filing-period signal is constrained by what was published and when it was available. A market-price signal reflects a separate repricing process, where such data are authorized for use. A model tier is a derivation from published components: a published rule over published components, not an independent observation. It is not a credit rating, a finding of fraud, or a prediction of default.

The India construction record covers 48 institutions across two decades and reports 88.9% of non-fraud failures flagged, with median lead of 21.5 months. First Leasing belongs at the boundary of that result, not inside a comforting reading of it. The case is fraud-masked, while the headline result specifically concerns non-fraud failures. The market evidence index retains the construction label and the eligibility limitations.

The strongest counter-case

The strongest counter-case can defeat an inflated thesis. A fraud-masked label does not prove that ordinary balance-sheet thresholds were useless, nor that a forensic overlay would have supplied a timely and usable warning. The dossier does not provide the First Leasing filing series needed to reconstruct a discrepancy line by line. It also does not establish a causal sequence beyond the default date, the fraud-masked classification, and the absence of documented first signals in these lenses.

The broader model evidence does not cure that gap. The hazard panel has 205 rows, 9 events, and 27 institutions; 179 censored or unusable rows were excluded. Its leave-one-institution-out row AUC is 0.752, with a confidence interval from 0.338 to 1.0. The comparator heuristic records 0.799 on the same held-out rows. The temporal diagnostic AUC is 0.645, below the 0.65 gate, and is diagnostic only.

The counter-case therefore lands: this replay cannot validate a timely warning for First Leasing. It may be no more than an example of a screen lacking the inputs needed to see a hidden problem. But that conclusion does not establish calm. Missing mechanical signals cannot validate the absence of underlying stress where disclosure integrity is itself at issue.

The next falsifiable test

This thesis fails if prospective, independently verified records show that forensic exceptions add no useful warning beyond action-zone and funding measures. It also fails if reconciled disclosures show that the fraud-masked designation does not distinguish cases in which threshold inputs were materially misleading.

A meaningful test requires publication-time source capture rather than period-end proxies, auditable asset and liability series, explicit missing-data treatment, and a prospectively frozen forensic rule. It must preserve the distinction between a filing-period observation, a market-price observation, and a model derivation. Until then, the responsible output is uncertainty, not a claim of detection skill.

Follow the pressure chain

The research boundary is explicit. Seiche: system dollar-funding capacity. LiquiLens: institution and lender balance-sheet risk. Undertow: market liquidity and executable exit capacity. These are different observations with different failure modes.

Seiche’s system view is context only for this purpose; it is not used in the institution score. Undertow’s market-liquidity board concerns executable exit capacity, not whether a lender’s disclosed balance sheet is accurate. Plumbing pressure, institution risk, and exit conditions may interact, but none substitutes for another.

Sources, method, and limits

The supplied sources are the Failure Radar board, historical validation, market evidence, the First Leasing case file, and LiquiLens research.

The methodology does not present quarterly filings older than 183 days or annual filings older than 400 days as current. Failed institutions are replayed on the Evidence tab instead. Components are replayed independently on the 48-institution construction-PIT record and fused only for presentation. The conformal alarm remains diagnostic and has no score or tier authority pending prospective revalidation.

Stale, future-dated, or unclocked market readings can remain visible but have no signal or tier authority. In this case, market-derived display is not approved for every expected canonical record. Those safeguards limit what the system claims to know; they do not repair falsified inputs. That is the durable lesson of First Leasing’s replay. Research and market data, not investment advice.
