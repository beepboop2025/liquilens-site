Sambandh Finserve’s funding flag arrived early, but did not make failure knowable

A fragile wholesale-leverage reading preceded Sambandh Finserve’s recorded default. It was a filing-period vulnerability signal, not a market-price verdict, calibrated failure probability, or forecast for institutions on today’s board.

1435 words · institution risk · open evidence

This historical replay is not current news and not a forecast. Sambandh Finserve’s recorded default date is 2020-10-13. Its relevant funding observation ended on 2016-03-31, and the record assigns a 2016-05-30 knowledge-time proxy. The Failure Radar is as of 2026-08-24, while its market layer is as of 2026-08-11.

The narrow answer to LiquiLens’s desk question is Sambandh Finserve: its disclosed liability structure produced the earliest recorded warning in this replay. Wholesale leverage was 8.51x against a 7x flag. That is a filing-period signal. It is not a contemporaneous market-price signal, because the dossier provides no quoted-market observation for Sambandh. Nor is it a model-derived failure probability. Its 52-month recorded lead makes it evidence of visible vulnerability, not a countdown to default.

Sambandh is fraud-masked. That limitation is central, not incidental. The dossier warns that filings later shown to be falsified can make an institution appear compliant to a threshold engine. Public disclosures may therefore be incomplete or distorted when a retrospective account is most tempted to sound certain.

The record before the event

Sambandh’s funding lens was scoreable because it disclosed the liability series needed for that lens. At the 2016-03-31 period end, its funding index was 78.7 and its band was fragile. The stated flag was wholesale leverage of 8.51x against the 7x threshold.

No explicit publication clock is supplied for this observation. The availability convention is explicit publication time when present; otherwise it is period end plus 60 days. That convention yields the 2016-05-30 knowledge-time proxy. The supportable conclusion is limited: by that proxy date, a published-data rule classified the disclosed liability structure as fragile.

The record does not establish when creditors repriced Sambandh, changed terms, or withdrew funding. It does not document asset sales, loss recognition, management action, or a causal path from leverage to default. Those could be subjects for further reporting, but they are not evidence contained here. The later recorded event occurred on 2020-10-13.

What the lenses saw

The funding lens was Sambandh’s first recorded signal. In the historical funding summary, 15 failed institutions are included, while 10 have liability disclosures. The funding signal fired first for 4, with a median lead of 38 months. This is a descriptive result conditional on liability disclosure. It neither shows that funding data are available for every institution nor proves that funding stress is the first route into every failure.

The PCA replay supplies no comparable Sambandh action-zone finding or lead measure. Across its summary, 15 failed institutions are included, 5 entered an action zone first, and median lead was 41 months. Missing PCA fields cannot demonstrate calm, especially for a fraud-masked institution. The PCA replay tracks disclosed supervisory tripwires; the forensic screen addresses a separate failure mode.

Model evidence requires its own label. The hazard panel contains 205 rows, 9 events, and 27 institutions; 179 censored or unusable rows were excluded. Its leave-one-institution-out AUC is 0.752, with an interval from 0.338 to 1.0. On the same held-out rows, the heuristic AUC is 0.799. The temporal diagnostic AUC is 0.645, below the 0.65 gate, and it did not pass. The conformal alarm remains diagnostic only: historical tier wiring is suspended, and it has no score or tier authority pending prospective revalidation.

Those are model derivations and validation diagnostics, not market-price judgements about Sambandh. The validation record and market-evidence index provide the relevant construction record.

Why the warning mattered

The wholesale-leverage flag identifies a visible liability-side sensitivity. It does not say default was inevitable or specify when it would occur. At most, the filing supports a conditional hypothesis: wholesale-funding dependence can become a pressure point if funding conditions change before the balance sheet adjusts. The ratio is structural evidence, not proof that such a change was already under way.

That distinction matters because hindsight can turn an early ratio into an apparently complete explanation. This dossier supports only the opening link: a fragile funding classification and a stated leverage flag. It does not establish the subsequent pressure chain.

The dates belong to separate evidentiary clocks. The balance-sheet observation belongs to 2016-03-31; the public-availability proxy belongs to 2016-05-30; and the recorded event belongs to 2020-10-13. A market-price signal would require a contemporaneous price observation. A model derivation belongs to a training and validation record. Combining those categories would overstate what any lens knew at the time.

The strongest counter-case

The counter-case defeats a claim that this was a timely prediction. A 52-month lead is long enough to show that the flag was not a near-term timing instrument. An institution can breach a broad funding threshold and continue operating. The historical record is labelled PERIOD_END_PROXY_CONSTRUCTION_PIT. It is not validated-backtest eligible, not real-money eligible, and has no bitemporal input contract. The dossier explicitly says lead times are optimistic.

Fraud masking strengthens rather than softens that objection. If filings were falsified, no public threshold engine had a complete view of the underlying balance sheet. The lack of a Sambandh PCA finding cannot establish resilience. It also cannot prove that funding uniquely explained the outcome; it is missing comparison evidence.

A cautious reader can accept the funding structure as an early, observable vulnerability and still reject the claim that public disclosures diagnosed the full event or made the default knowable.

The next falsifiable test

The funding thesis should fail if genuinely publication-timed future records show wholesale-funding flags do not precede independently observable funding pressure, while institutions without such flags repeatedly show pressure first. It should also fail as a timing claim if prospective cases show no useful ordering between a funding flag and later stress.

A proper test must preserve publication times, revisions, missing filings, and the fraud-masking boundary. It must assess future observations rather than fit known defaults. Until then, Sambandh remains a historical clue from a construction-PIT replay, not validated real-money evidence.
