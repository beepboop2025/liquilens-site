New India Co-operative Bank showed a disclosed stress breach before the event, and the fraud boundary is the point

A historical replay finds an NNPA action-zone breach at the FY2021 period end, ahead of the later default date. It supports investigation of disclosed balance-sheet pressure, not confidence that a threshold screen can see through false accounts.

1370 words · institution risk · open evidence

*This is a historical replay, not current news and not a forecast. It examines the public record before New India Co-operative Bank’s default date of 2025-02-13. Similar mechanisms do not imply the same outcome elsewhere.*

The desk question has a conditional answer. On the disclosed record, New India Co-operative Bank should have drawn attention through an asset-quality signal: an NNPA breach placing it in the RBI PCA/SAF action zone for the period ended 2021-03-31, labelled FY2021. The replay uses 2021-05-30 as its knowledge-time proxy and records a 44-month lead to default.

That is a filing-period signal. It is not proof that the published balance sheet described the institution’s actual condition, and it is not a market-price signal or a model result. The dossier marks this case fraud-masked. That qualification is decisive: a reported threshold crossing warrants investigation, but cannot deliver a forensic account of a bank whose filings may have been unreliable.

The record before the event

Three clocks must remain separate. The filing period ended on 2021-03-31. Where no explicit publication clock is available, the replay uses period end plus 60 days, producing the 2021-05-30 knowledge-time proxy. The default occurred on 2025-02-13. None is a current-market timestamp, or evidence of what management, supervisors, depositors, or counterparties knew privately.

The PCA/SAF lens replays public disclosure against the RBI’s action-zone tripwires. It can identify a reported threshold breach, not establish causation, reconstruct undisclosed cash movements, assess intent, or verify that the reported inputs were true. The New India replay is evidence of what a disclosure screen could flag from the available record.

Fraud-masked is not a footnote. Institutions whose filings were later shown falsified can appear compliant to a threshold engine. Even this early visible breach cannot establish whether reported deterioration captured the full problem, its first stage, or only part of it. A disclosure warning can be useful and materially incomplete at once.

What the lenses saw

The PCA/SAF lens saw the NNPA breach. The funding lens produced no first signal for New India. That is missing evidence, not evidence of stable funding: the funding lens only sees institutions that disclose a liability series. No funding signal cannot become a funding all-clear.

Across 15 failed institutions, 5 entered the PCA/SAF action zone first, with a median lead of 41 months. The funding summary has liability disclosures for 10 of those 15 institutions; its signal fired first for 4, with a median lead of 38 months. These are replay descriptions, not prospective accuracy claims.

The wider India diagnostic covers 48 institutions across two decades and says 88.9% of non-fraud failures were flagged, with a median lead of 21.5 months. Yet it is explicitly PERIOD_END_PROXY_CONSTRUCTION_PIT: not eligible as a validated backtest or real-money test, without a bitemporal input contract, and with optimistic lead times. The validation record and market evidence index retain those limits.

A model derivation is separate again. The hazard panel contains 205 rows, 9 events, and 27 institutions. Its leave-one-institution-out AUC is 0.752, while the heuristic reaches 0.799 on the same held-out rows. Its temporal diagnostic AUC is 0.645, below the 0.65 gate, and remains diagnostic only. Those results describe a constrained construction exercise; they do not show that New India was predictably destined to fail.

Why the warning mattered

A disclosed action-zone breach changes the question from whether an institution looks routine to whether reported asset quality, loss absorption, disclosure quality, and possible funding vulnerability warrant deeper scrutiny.

If reported condition is genuine, pressure may move from asset quality into loss absorption, confidence, funding, and resilience to further losses. But that is a pressure chain, not a demonstrated causal sequence here. New India has an asset-side warning and no funding first signal. Its fraud-masked designation means disclosures may not have measured the actual condition fully. Investigation, rather than a tidy narrative of linear deterioration, is the warranted inference.

The 44-month lead is both meaningful and hazardous. It identifies an early public point at which the disclosed record crossed a regulatory threshold. But a long replay lead can make a threshold engine appear to have seen more than historical filings could reveal.

The strongest counter-case

The counter-case can defeat any broad thesis. New India does not establish that disclosed thresholds reliably forecast institutional failure. An NNPA breach is not proof of insolvency, liquidity failure, misconduct, or an impending default. Fraud-masked cases sharpen the objection: a system built from public disclosures cannot claim forensic sight when the disclosures themselves may be unreliable.

Timing is another objection. The replay uses explicit publication time when present and otherwise a period-end-plus-60-days proxy. That can make lead times optimistic. Without a bitemporal input contract, the record cannot prove what a live user could access on each historical date. The conformal alarm is diagnostic, with no score or tier authority pending prospective revalidation.

A later event also does not retroactively prove decision usefulness. The heuristic’s stronger held-out AUC and the failed temporal gate limit the model conclusion. The defensible thesis is narrower: disclosed action-zone pressure is a reason to investigate earlier, while fraud and missing disclosures limit what the investigation can infer.

The next falsifiable test

The thesis weakens if institutions crossing disclosed action-zone thresholds do not later show persistent, independently disclosed balance-sheet pressure more often than comparable institutions that do not cross them. It also weakens if prospective testing with actual publication timestamps, rather than period-end proxies, fails to retain useful separation.

A persuasive test must distinguish a filing-period signal from a market-price signal and from a model derivation. It must establish what was public, when it was public, whether it was accurate, and whether it added information beyond simpler disclosure rules. A later event alone cannot satisfy those tests.
