Sambandh Finserve’s early stress signal was funding, not a forecast

A historical replay places a fragile wholesale-liability signal before Sambandh Finserve’s default. The lesson is a balance-sheet mechanism to investigate, not a validated prediction or a market verdict.

1352 words · institution risk · open evidence

*This is a historical replay, not current news and not a forecast. It examines information associated with Sambandh Finserve before its 2020-10-13 default. Publication date: 2026-09-17. The relevant filing period ended 2016-03-31, with a knowledge-time proxy of 2016-05-30. Similar mechanisms do not imply the same outcome elsewhere.*

The narrow thesis is that Sambandh Finserve’s balance sheet should have felt stress first through funding. Wholesale leverage could leave refinancing capacity exposed to a change in lenders’ willingness to roll liabilities. That is not a claim that a ratio forecast the default, that the outcome was fully knowable, or that the screen measured a probability of failure. It is a claim about where disclosed balance-sheet pressure appeared earliest and where diligence should have concentrated.

A filing-period signal is an observation in disclosed accounts. A market-price signal is a contemporaneous repricing measure. A model derivation combines inputs under stated rules. They are not interchangeable evidence. Sambandh’s signal is a filing-period observation replayed with an availability proxy. This article makes no market-derived institutional conclusion.

The record before the event

Sambandh was scoreable in the funding lens. Its first signal was attached to 2016-03-31: an index of 78.7, in the fragile band, with wholesale leverage of 8.51x against a 7x threshold. Where an explicit publication clock is unavailable, the historical record uses period end plus 60 days. That produces the knowledge-time proxy of 2016-05-30 and a stated lead of 52 months before default.

There are material absences. No first PCA action-zone event is recorded, and neither an action-zone date nor a PCA lead is available. The institution is fraud-masked. That is not cosmetic: filings later shown to have been falsified can look compliant to a threshold engine. The dossier assigns that failure mode to a forensic screen. A liability ratio can flag a disclosed vulnerability without establishing that the disclosure was reliable.

The historical status is PERIOD_END_PROXY_CONSTRUCTION_PIT. The input contract is not bitemporal. The replay is not eligible as a validated backtest or as real-money evidence, and its lead times are explicitly described as optimistic. The dated sequence is available in the Sambandh replay and the wider replay archive. It cannot prove that an analyst possessed every relevant input at the apparent lead date.

What the lenses saw

The funding lens saw a liability-side warning. The PCA replay did not record an initial action-zone event for Sambandh. That supports a limited statement: in this supplied record, the earliest disclosed concern came through funding rather than an RBI PCA/SAF tripwire. It does not show that funding is generally earlier, more accurate, or more useful for every institution.

Across the failed-institution summaries, PCA covers 15 institutions, with 5 entering an action zone first and a median lead of 41 months. Funding also covers 15 failed institutions, but only 10 had liability disclosures; the funding signal fired first for 4, with a median lead of 38 months. Coverage is decisive. A funding lens cannot identify a liability condition when the necessary series is not disclosed.

The India diagnostic covers 48 institutions across two decades and reports 88.9% of non-fraud failures flagged, with median lead of 21.5 months. Yet it has the same filing-availability-proxied construction-PIT status, no bitemporal input contract, and no validated-backtest or real-money eligibility. This is diagnostic evidence, not demonstrated deployable performance. Readers can inspect the Failure Radar board, historical validation, and research archive.

Why the warning mattered

Wholesale leverage matters as a possible transmission channel, not as a complete explanation. A liability-heavy funding structure can leave balance-sheet flexibility sensitive to refinancing appetite and terms. The disclosed ratio therefore raised a question: could the liability base withstand less accommodating lender behaviour?

The 8.51x observation does not answer that question. It does not identify counterparties, maturity concentrations, management actions, the timing of crystallisation, or the cause of the eventual default. It identifies where further evidence would have been most valuable.

Calling this a funding signal rather than a forecast preserves the evidentiary order. The ratio was observed under a published rule. The 60-day clock is a construction assumption when publication timing is absent. The 52-month lead is a model-derived interval, not proof of a tradable or actionable advance warning.

The strongest counter-case

The counter-case can defeat the thesis. This may be retrospective coincidence amplified by an assumed availability clock. Because the lead begins at period end and uses a 60-day proxy where necessary, the apparent 52 months may overstate what a contemporaneous analyst could have known. The dossier calls these leads optimistic.

The statistical record is not a clean endorsement. The hazard panel has 205 rows, 9 events, and 27 institutions, while 179 censored or unusable rows were excluded. Its leave-one-institution-out AUC is 0.752, with an interval from 0.338 to 1.0. The disclosure heuristic recorded 0.799 on the same held-out rows. The temporal diagnostic reported 0.645 against a 0.65 gate and failed it; it remains diagnostic only.

Fraud masking sharpens the challenge. If accounts can be unreliable, an early ratio flag cannot be credited with seeing the whole case. Equally, an absent flag cannot be read as safety. The fair conclusion is only that funding fragility was an early disclosed concern for Sambandh, not that the model validated a prediction of default.

The next falsifiable test

The thesis fails if properly clocked prospective disclosures show that high wholesale leverage does not precede measurable refinancing pressure, lending restraint, or liability-side deterioration more often than comparable institutions without the flag. It also fails if a bitemporal dataset using actual publication availability loses useful discrimination once the construction-PIT proxy is removed.

A credible test requires fresh vetted public dossiers, explicit publication clocks, disclosed liability series, and a pre-specified rule applied before outcomes. Fraud-masked cases must remain separate. Until then, this is diagnostic scrutiny, not confidence.
